Constructivism is the Piagetian thesis that development proceeds by learning mechanisms that yield qualitatively new kinds of representation—for example, that a child might start without the idea that occluded objects persist and, through experience, come to represent object permanence. The hard problem for constructivism has always been a concrete computational story of how learning alone produces those new representational types.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus’s question is whether connectionist models—especially those advertised in *Rethinking Innateness*—can rescue constructivism by showing how networks invent new representational formats from experience. He answers no: existing connectionist accounts do not supply the missing mechanism for genuine representational change of the Piagetian sort, and they do not dissolve the need for prior structure. The term is therefore used critically: constructivism remains aspirational without an adequate learning theory, and “connectionism saves constructivism” is treated as a claim that has not been redeemed, preserving space for nativist constraints on what learning can build.
