Overregularization is the developmental phenomenon in which children extend regular grammatical patterns to irregular words—producing forms such as *comed*, *goed*, or *mouses*—often after an earlier period of correct irregular use (the classic U-shaped trajectory). The errors show creative rule application interacting with memorized exceptions, and they raise the problem of how overgeneral rules are later unlearned without dense explicit correction.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> For Marcus (with Pinker and colleagues), overregularization is paradigmatic evidence of mental rules, not mere analogy or surface pattern matching. It does work in his framework as a developmental signature of algebraic, structure-sensitive morphology: a default regular process applies when memory retrieval of an irregular fails. That rules out accounts that treat all inflection as one undifferentiated associative map, and it departs from purely input-driven readings of the errors by tying them to the architecture of rules-plus-memory rather than to gradual statistical tuning alone.
