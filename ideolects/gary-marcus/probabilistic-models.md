Probabilistic models of higher-level cognition are theories that cast the mind as an engine of Bayesian or near-optimal probabilistic inference across domains such as word learning, pragmatics, naive physics, and prediction. They typically identify rational inference under uncertainty with probabilistic computation, and treat observed human judgments as approximations to posterior inference in a generative model of the task.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> With Davis, Marcus argues that this Bayesian-as-default picture is far less robust than its popularity suggests. Many published successes depend on post hoc model choices among multiple equally plausible Bayesian formulations, so the “fit” does not uniquely support the framework; moreover, equating *probabilistic* with *rational* or *optimal* is unjustified. The term is used to constrain methodological hype: statistical structure in cognition is real, but near-optimal probabilistic inference is not an automatic explanation of higher thought. It rules out treating Bayesian formalisms as unfalsifiable after-the-fact rationalizations, and it keeps room for structured, sometimes non-optimal mechanisms that his broader architecture favors.
