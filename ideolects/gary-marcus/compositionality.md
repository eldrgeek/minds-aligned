Compositionality is the principle that the meaning or form of a complex expression is determined by its parts and the grammatical relations among them—binding, coreference, recursion, and other structure-sensitive processes that recombine known elements into novel wholes. In human language and thought, it underwrites productive generalization: once the pieces and the combinatorial rules are available, indefinitely many new sentences and scenes can be constructed and understood.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus deploys compositionality as a stress test for deep generative systems. Analyses of DALL·E 2 (with Leivada, Murphy, and others) show unreliable capture of common syntactic processes that humans treat compositionally, undercutting claims that large models have acquired human-like linguistic competence. The term rules out equating photorealistic or fluent output with structural understanding, and it departs from purely distributional readings of “compositionality” by treating hierarchical, grammar-sensitive combination—not approximate co-occurrence collage—as the standard a robust cognitive or AI system must meet.
