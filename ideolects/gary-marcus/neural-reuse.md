Neural reuse is the thesis that brain circuits evolved or developed for one purpose are routinely recruited for additional, often quite different cognitive functions. Rather than each capacity having an exclusive neural substrate, the same populations and pathways participate in multiple domains—language, number, social cognition, and so on—through redeployment of existing computational machinery.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus and Rabaglia endorse neural reuse as highly plausible and use individual-difference structure—especially the positive manifold, in which abilities correlate across domains—as further evidence for it. In his framework, reuse supports a non-caricatured nativism: innate machinery can be multipurpose and historically recycled rather than a set of sealed, one-function organs. What it rules out is strict one-module-one-circuit modularity and the idea that domain-specificity always requires dedicated wetware. The departure from a purely neuroanatomical reading is functional-evolutionary: reuse is not just overlapping activation maps but part of how minds get complex structure without perfect design—compatible with kluges, descent with modification, and hybrid cognitive architecture.
