Cognitive architecture is the overall organization of the mind’s mechanisms—how its parts develop, whether they are modular, how optimal they are, and whether they operate as symbol-manipulating systems or as purely associative networks. For Marcus, architecture is not a flowchart of tasks but a biologically informed account of the machinery that makes those tasks possible.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus uses developmental and evolutionary biology to force compromises in historically polarized debates: the mind is neither a blank slate nor a set of perfect, sealed modules; it is structured yet imperfect, partially specialized, and capable of symbol manipulation implemented in neural tissue. Architecture talk rules out pure performance descriptions that never ask *how* cognition works, and it rules out both extreme massive modularity and extreme eliminative connectionism. Distinctively, he treats the mind as a biological kluge-like system whose design history constrains what a realistic architecture can look like—hybrid, descent-with-modification, and not engineered from first principles for global optimality.
