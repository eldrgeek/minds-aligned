Rules and similarity name two modes of generalization: rule-based operations apply abstract, structure-sensitive operations over variables (as in regular inflection or algebraic patterns), while similarity-based processes generalize from resemblance among stored exemplars or statistical neighborhoods. Marcus treats both as real cognitive resources that can be studied empirically in language, reasoning, and learning.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus's distinctive claim is that criteria aimed at dichotomizing cognition into "rules versus similarity" are destined to fail because the two are not in genuine conflict. A domain can exploit rules without similarity, similarity without rules, or both at once. What this rules out is the standard continuum framing—common in debates over connectionism and dual-route morphology—in which more of one means less of the other. In his framework, the dual-mechanism picture (rules for regulars, memory/similarity for irregulars) is compatible with hybrid architecture rather than a forced choice; statistical learning and symbol manipulation are complementary tools, not rival monisms.
