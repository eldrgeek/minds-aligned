Negative evidence is information that a learner’s utterance is ungrammatical—parental corrections, recasts, or other feedback that marks errors as errors. In the language-acquisition literature Marcus engages, the question is whether children receive enough reliable negative evidence, and of the right kind, to acquire the adult grammar by pure empiricist induction from experience.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus treats the scarcity and unreliability of explicit negative evidence as a structural constraint on learning theories, not as a minor empirical detail. If children do not systematically get told which strings are out, then mechanisms that depend on external error signals—including many simple recurrent networks and purely statistical learners—cannot by themselves explain how abstract grammatical generalizations are fixed. The term rules out “just more feedback” stories of acquisition and supports his broader nativist and rule-oriented stance: learners must bring internal constraints and representational structure that allow productive generalization without exhaustive correction.
