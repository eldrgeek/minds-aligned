Modularity is the thesis that cognitive systems can be organized into relatively specialized components—distinct functional units with constrained inputs, outputs, and developmental trajectories—rather than a single fully general processor. In developmental debates, patterns of spared and impaired abilities are often taken as evidence for or against such specialization.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus focuses less on celebrating Fodorian modules than on the *logic* of inference: observed developmental-disorder profiles do not, by themselves, prove or disprove modular organization. The term, in his hands, rules out simplistic double-dissociation arguments that read architecture directly off clinical patterns, and it departs from both strong mass-action anti-modularity and naive one-module-per-deficit nativism by insisting that modularity claims require careful mapping between genes, development, and computation—not just selective impairment.
