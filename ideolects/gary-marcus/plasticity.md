Plasticity, in Marcus's usage, is the nervous system's capacity to reorganize, rewire, and retune itself in response to experience, injury, and environmental demand—without thereby proving that the mind is a blank slate. It names real developmental and neural flexibility: sensitive periods, experience-dependent tuning, and post-injury reorganization that show brains are not rigidly hard-wired maps of fixed functions.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus treats plasticity as the apparent foil to nativism that, properly understood, does not refute innateness. In his framing, the paradox dissolves once innate structure is seen as providing initial machinery, constraints, and learning biases that plasticity then elaborates—rather than as a denial that learning and reorganization occur. What this rules out is the common inference from plasticity evidence to pure empiricism or eliminative connectionism: flexibility of implementation is not absence of built-in architecture. The distinctive move is to make plasticity and nativism jointly necessary rather than mutually exclusive—plasticity operates over, and is partly shaped by, innate structure.
