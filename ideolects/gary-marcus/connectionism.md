Connectionism is the research tradition that models mind and learning with networks of interconnected units whose strengths change with experience, often as an alternative to classical symbol systems. In Marcus’s debates of the 1990s and after, the live issue is not whether brains are networks, but whether connectionist models can capture human generalization *without* abstract rules, variables, and structured operations.

<strong>Gary Marcus's lens:</strong> Marcus refuses a binary “networks versus symbols” framing. He accepts that biological cognition is neurally implemented, yet insists that infant rule learning and universal generalization implicate algebra-like machinery that many pure associationist networks lack. Against McClelland, Plaut, and related “cognition-without-rules” views, he treats successful connectionism as that which *implements* rules and structured representations, not that which eliminates them. The term thus separates implementational neural modeling (compatible with his algebraic mind) from eliminative programs that treat rules as optional or illusory.
