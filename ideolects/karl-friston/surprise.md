Surprise (self-information) is the negative log probability of an outcome under a probabilistic model—how unexpected a sensory exchange is given the agent's expectations. Organisms that remain within a limited repertoire of characteristic states must, over the long run, avoid surprising exchanges with their environment. Because true surprise is not directly evaluable without knowing the marginal likelihood of sensations, agents instead minimize a tractable upper bound—variational free energy—whose minimization is equivalent to maximizing Bayesian model evidence.

<strong>Karl Friston's lens:</strong> Surprise here is not the psychological startle of an unexpected event, nor Shannon surprise computed by an external observer with the true world distribution. It is surprise *under the agent's own generative model*—the quantity living systems must keep low if they are to persist as bounded, ergodic entities. Free energy is the bound that makes that imperative computable; self-evidencing is the dual description in which avoiding surprise is maximizing evidence for one's own model of being. That framing rules out treating surprise purely as a transient arousal signal and anchors it as the thermodynamic–statistical quantity that free-energy minimization is always about.
