The free energy principle is Friston's claim that any self-organizing system that remains far from thermodynamic equilibrium—paradigmatically a brain, more generally an agent bounded by a Markov blanket—must minimize a variational free-energy bound on surprise (the negative log probability of its sensory exchanges). Free energy is an upper bound on sensory surprise under a generative model of how hidden causes produce observations; minimizing it is formally equivalent to maximizing Bayesian model evidence, so adaptive systems look as if they are performing approximate Bayesian inference about the causes of their sensory input.

<strong>Karl Friston's lens:</strong> Friston does not treat free energy as a metaphorical "energy" of neural computation, nor as a cost function chosen by the modeller. It is a necessary quantity that any ergodic system with a Markov blanket will appear to minimize if it is to occupy a limited set of characteristic states over time. That reframing rules out accounts in which perception and action are separately optimized objectives, and it demotes classical reward or utility to special cases of prior preferences encoded in the generative model. The principle is therefore metaphysical and thermodynamic in ambition—an account of how adaptive systems must behave—rather than a narrow psychological theory of Bayesian brains.
