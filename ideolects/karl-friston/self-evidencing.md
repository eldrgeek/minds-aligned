Self-evidencing is the description of adaptive, self-organizing systems as creatures that continually maximize the evidence for their own generative model of the world—including a model of themselves as distinct from their niche. Because model evidence is the negative of surprise, a system that returns to a limited set of attracting (characteristic) states is, mathematically, accumulating evidence that it is the kind of thing that occupies those states. Self-organization under a Markov blanket is thereby rewritten as inference: every process that maintains the boundary and its internal dynamics is a form of self-evidencing.

<strong>Karl Friston's lens:</strong> Friston uses self-evidencing to collapse the gap between thermodynamic persistence and Bayesian inference. Living systems do not first exist and then optionally do inference; their very existence *is* evidence accumulation for a model that predicts the sensory consequences of being that system. That rules out treating "survival" and "belief updating" as separate agendas, and it departs from ordinary philosophy-of-mind talk of self-models as optional higher-order representations. For Friston, selfhood begins where a Markov blanket licenses a partition into internal states that look as if they are inferring—and thereby evidencing—themselves.
