Ambiguity, in Friston's active-inference decomposition of expected free energy, is the expected inaccuracy or conditional uncertainty of outcomes given hidden states under a policy—roughly, how unresolvable or noisy the mapping from states to observations is anticipated to be. Policies that reduce ambiguity are epistemically valuable: they seek observations that make states clearer, enabling later pragmatic success.

<strong>Karl Friston's lens:</strong> Friston pairs ambiguity with risk as the two complementary faces of expected free energy: explorative (ambiguity-resolving) behaviour enables exploitative (risk-sensitive, preference-seeking) behaviour and the subsequent formation of habits. Ambiguity is not colloquial vagueness nor Knightian unknown-unknowns alone; it is a precise term of art for expected conditional entropy in the generative model of outcomes. That rules out treating exploration as an ad hoc bonus on reward, and it makes information-seeking mandatory under free-energy minimization whenever the agent can choose policies that render the world less equivocal—until habits render belief-based deliberation unnecessary in well-learned contexts.
