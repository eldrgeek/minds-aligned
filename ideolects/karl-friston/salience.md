Salience is the score attached to candidate actions (or policies) according to how much they are expected to resolve uncertainty—how strongly they realise an epistemic affordance. Under active inference it is formalised through expected free energy: policies that yield information gain about hidden states or contingencies are more salient than those that do not. Salience therefore selects *where to sample next* (which observation to solicit), not which sensory channel to amplify once data have arrived.

<strong>Karl Friston's lens:</strong> Friston (with Parr) insists on a hard distinction that everyday psychology collapses: attention is the precision-weighting of prediction errors already in play, whereas salience is prior to sampling—it scores policies that will make data informative. That rules out treating salience as mere stimulus “pop-out” or as a synonym for attention, and it relocates the construct from sensory features to action under a generative model. Salience becomes the epistemic half of policy selection: the formal reason an agent looks, orients, or forages before it updates beliefs about what it has seen.
