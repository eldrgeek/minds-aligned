An observer, in Fields's usage, is any finite physical system—human, apparatus, or other agent—that distinguishes a system of interest from its environment by encoding a finite set of classical identifying data (for example reference eigenvalues) and then recording pointer outcomes relative to that identification. Observation is not a passive readout of a pre-given object; it is an interaction in which the observer implements a partition, holds the resources that make the partition usable, and thereby participates in what counts as system, environment, and measurement record.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields treats the observer as an information-limited participant whose system–environment boundary must be stipulated and paid for thermodynamically, not as an idealized external vantage point. Decoherence itself is rewritten as a sequence of entanglement swaps among reference and pointer components once that identifying information is made explicit. This rules out semi-classical models that assume ontic boundaries free of cost or encoding, and it departs from textbook readings in which the observer is either a classical external recorder or a pure formal posit. For Fields, every observer is itself a finite quantum system with quantum reference frames, so multi-observer communication, redundancy, and apparent classicality become problems of shared identification rather than of collapse by an outside mind.
