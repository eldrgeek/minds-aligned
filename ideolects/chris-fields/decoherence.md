Decoherence is the process by which a quantum system comes to look classical to an observer who can distinguish it from its environment. In Fields's formulation, that distinction itself is informational: any finite observer or apparatus must encode a finite set of reference eigenvalues that specify the system–environment boundary, and only relative to those reference data do pointer states become robust classical records.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Standard semi-classical decoherence stories presuppose an ontic cut between system and environment and then let environment-induced superselection do the rest. Fields instead represents the missing classical information of system identification explicitly, and shows that decoherence can be rewritten as a sequence of entanglement swaps between reference and pointer components of the system and their respective environments. This rules out treating the boundary as given independently of observation, and it departs from textbook open-systems accounts that never charge the observer for the bits required to say what is being decohered. Classical appearance is therefore conditional on successful system identification, not a free gift of large environments alone.
