Quantum Darwinism is the proposal that classical objectivity emerges when many observers independently sample redundant environmental encodings of a macroscopic quantum system's state. In that picture, the environment acts as a broadcast channel: pointer information about system S is copied into fragments of E, so remote observers can agree on what they see without directly disturbing S.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields grants the environmental-encoding idea but denies that it yields a purely quantum explanation of classical emergence. Multiple observers who interact only with environmental encodings of S cannot demonstrate that they are jointly observing the same S unless they already share an a priori classical boundary separating S from E. Quantum Darwinism therefore cannot determine classical system boundaries from within; it presupposes the cut that classicality was supposed to explain. This rules out reading redundant environmental records as sufficient for intersubjective classicality, and it departs from Zurek-style narratives in which objectivity falls out of decoherence plus redundancy alone. For Fields, shared classicality requires jointly stipulated identification structure—reference data, POVMs, or holographic screens—not only prolific copies in the environment.
