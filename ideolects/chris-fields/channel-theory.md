Channel Theory is the Barwise–Seligman framework in which distributed systems share information along channels regulated by local logics and classifications, often paired with Chu spaces as a categorical presentation of objects, attributes, and satisfaction. In Fields's usage it is the formal language for semantically structured information flow: observational outcomes, object files, and mereological part–whole structure are modeled as classifiers linked by channels rather than as bare bit strings.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields and Glazebrook deploy Channel Theory not as abstract logic but as a working model of how finite observers identify objects, track identity through time, and build hierarchical Bayesian inferences under context. Classifications and local logics constrain which tokens can be re-identified as the “same” individual; cone–cocone diagrams over classifiers become a generic representation of quantum reference frames and sequential measurement. This rules out treating meaning as a post hoc gloss on Shannon channels, and it departs from standard quantum-information readings in which classical communication is mere LOCC wiring. For Fields, the channel carries semantics: the same architecture that underwrites visual object identification also underwrites holographic screens as classical information channels between quantum subsystems.
