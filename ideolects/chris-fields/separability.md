Separability is the assumption that a joint physical situation can be factored into independent subsystems with their own state spaces, so that preparation and measurement on one factor do not intrinsically depend on the other. In Fields's usage it is the operational hinge between classical bookkeeping and quantum entanglement: observers must assume separability to identify systems and assign outcomes, even though the underlying joint state need not respect that cut.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields and Glazebrook link separability assumptions directly to preparation and measurement contextuality and to the frame problem—predicting what does *not* change after an action. Separability is not a free metaphysical postulate but a pragmatic commitment observers make when they allocate finite classical bits to system identification; when that commitment fails, contextuality appears and “what stayed the same” becomes undecidable in open domains. This rules out treating entanglement solely as a resource bolted onto already-identified systems, and it departs from standard readings that cleanly separate “system labels” from “state content.” For Fields, labels and content co-vary: dropping separability collapses the classical inventory of objects an agent can track.
