Decompositional equivalence is the principle that physical laws are invariant under changes in how the world is partitioned and labeled into systems. Both classical and quantum mechanics tacitly assume that which Hilbert-space factorization or classical cut one writes down does not alter the dynamics; Fields formalizes that assumption as a named principle and draws its consequences for measurement and system reidentification.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields shows that decompositional equivalence forbids finite experimental demonstrations that two pointer states refer to "the same" physical system S, and that any extra-theoretical assumption equating pointer states with indistinguishable Schmidt coefficients to one and the same S is stronger than assuming classicality. Standard measurement interpretations smuggle in such an assumption; hence they are logically stronger than bare quantum theory. This rules out treating system boundaries as free descriptive conveniences that leave physics unchanged while still licensing talk of reidentified systems across contexts, and it departs from textbook practice in which decomposition is regarded as arbitrary bookkeeping. Decompositional equivalence is why system identification, quantum reference frames, and observer-relative boundaries become unavoidable theoretical problems rather than optional philosophy.
