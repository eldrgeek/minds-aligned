System identification is the observer's problem of deciding which physical degrees of freedom—which Hilbert space, which boundary, which collection of pointer eigenvalues—constitute the “system” on which a measurement is being performed. In Fields's usage it is not a bookkeeping preamble to physics but a thermodynamically constrained, finite-resolution task: every real observer has only finite, noisy data with which to draw the system–environment cut, so the identified system is always approximate.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> Fields treats system identification as foundational and, under finite resources, unsolvable in exact form—equivalent, in his later framing, to the symbol-grounding problem. What standard quantum measurement theory takes as given (a pre-labeled system of interest and apparatus) he treats as something the observer must continually re-earn by paying a thermodynamic cost in free energy and classical bits. That cost forces separability assumptions that can fail, producing Bell/CHSH and Leggett–Garg violations as signatures of identification under strain rather than as exotic nonlocal miracles. The move rules out treating “the system” as an ontic primitive independent of observation, and it departs from textbook readings in which identification is external metaphysics while decoherence does the real work.
