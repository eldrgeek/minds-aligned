Contextuality is the failure of a single, context-independent assignment of values (or probabilities) to measurement and preparation outcomes across different experimental settings. In Fields's usage it is not only the Kochen–Specker or Bell-type phenomenon of quantum foundations but a structural feature of any finite observer who must choose bases, exchange free energy, and impose causal models in order to identify systems and predict what actions do and do not change.

<strong>Chris Fields's lens:</strong> With Glazebrook, Fields links assumptions of state separability to both preparation and measurement contextuality, and both of these to a quantum analog of the frame problem—the undecidable task of predicting what does not change after an action. Contextuality is generically induced by basis choice, thermodynamic exchange, and a priori causal models; settings that look “non-contextual” typically hide fine-tuning. This rules out treating classical, context-free value tables as the default from which quantum theory departs, and it departs from readings that confine contextuality to exotic lab tests. For Fields, contextuality is the everyday cost of being a finite, separable agent that must re-identify systems across changing interaction contexts.
