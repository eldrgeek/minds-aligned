Biological naturalism, in Seth's recent usage, is the thesis that consciousness depends on our nature as living organisms—on being the right kind of biological system—rather than on abstract computation alone. Applied to AI, it frames the question of machine consciousness as a question about whether artificial systems can instantiate the life-like and brain-like conditions that, on this view, underwrite conscious experience in animals including humans.

<strong>Anil Seth's lens:</strong> Seth deploys biological naturalism against the default assumption that sufficient intelligence, functional organization, or substrate-independent computation would automatically yield consciousness. He diagnoses biases that make conscious AI seem inevitable, challenges computational sufficiency, and maps scenarios in which artificial consciousness becomes more or less plausible as systems become more brain-like and/or life-like—concluding that genuine artificial consciousness is unlikely on current trajectories. This rules out strong functionalist and pure substrate-independence optimism about conscious AI, while remaining naturalistic (no dualist residue). It departs from John Searle's better-known "biological naturalism" mainly in emphasis and use: Seth's version is operationalized as a constraint on AI consciousness debates and is continuous with his beast-machine and interoceptive accounts of embodied selfhood, not primarily as a philosophy-of-mind slogan about neurons versus silicon.
