Causal density is a quantitative measure of dynamical complexity that summarizes how densely a system is filled with directed causal interactions among its elements over time. In Seth's work with colleagues it is proposed, alongside integrated information, as a candidate index of *conscious level*: high during vivid alert wakefulness, low in dreamless sleep, coma, and general anaesthesia, because conscious scenes are taken to require dynamics that are simultaneously differentiated and integrated.

<strong>Anil Seth's lens:</strong> Causal density is not a theory of *what* it is like to be conscious, nor a measure of particular conscious *contents*; it is an information-theoretic, Granger-causality-based probe of global dynamical structure relevant to level of consciousness. It operationalizes the joint requirement of differentiation (many distinct causal pathways) and integration (those pathways interact as a unified system), and is developed with an eye to practical application on time-series neural data. This rules out single-channel or purely correlational "complexity" scores that ignore directed interaction structure, and it sits apart from purely phenomenological or computational-functional definitions of consciousness. Where integrated information theory prioritizes intrinsic irreducibility of a system's cause-effect repertoire, Seth's causal density emphasis is more empirical and time-series native: a tool for comparing states of consciousness in brains rather than a metaphysical criterion of consciousness as such.
