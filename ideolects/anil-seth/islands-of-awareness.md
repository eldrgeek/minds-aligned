Islands of awareness are putative conscious states supported by brain activity that is fully causally isolated from the body and the wider environment—cortical territories that might still generate experience even though they neither receive ordinary sensorimotor traffic nor issue behavior. Bayne, Seth, and Massimini introduce the idea by surveying natural and artificial candidates (for example, disconnected hemispheres, isolated cortical slabs, or engineered organoids) and by framing the detection problem: how would one ever know that such an island is conscious if standard behavioral and sensorimotor criteria are unavailable?

<strong>Anil Seth's lens:</strong> For Seth the phrase is a stress test of theories of consciousness that smuggle in residual body–world coupling. It rules out equating consciousness with report, adaptive sensorimotor engagement, or whole-organism behavioral competence, and it forces a sharper distinction between local dynamical signatures of conscious level and the global, embodied setting in which ordinary awareness usually lives. Relative to everyday clinical talk of “covert awareness,” islands of awareness push further: not merely unresponsive patients with residual connectivity, but the conceptual possibility of awareness without any causal bridge to body or world—carrying ethical weight for disorders of consciousness, hemispherotomy, and future artificial systems.
