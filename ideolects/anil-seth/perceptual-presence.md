Perceptual presence is the property of ordinary perception by which objects in a scene are experienced as real—as existing in the world rather than as free-floating images, afterimages, or purely mental appearances. In Seth's account it is not a bare sensory quality but a structured phenomenological feature that sensorimotor and predictive theories of perception must explain, including why it can be reduced or absent in cases such as synesthesia.

<strong>Anil Seth's lens:</strong> Seth operationalizes presence via predictive processing of sensorimotor contingencies (PPSMC): generative models include explicitly counterfactual predictions about how sensory signals would change under possible actions, and mastery of those contingencies yields the sense of objecthood and worldliness. Presence is thus neither raw sensory vividness nor a simple belief that something is "out there"; it is the felt consequence of counterfactually rich, action-linked prediction. This rules out accounts that treat presence as mere signal strength or as identical to conscious access, and it explains synesthetic concurrents as percepts that lack the relevant counterfactual sensorimotor depth. Relative to classical sensorimotor contingency theory, Seth supplies a hierarchical Bayesian mechanism; relative to bare predictive coding, he insists presence requires counterfactual action structure, not only feedforward hypothesis testing about current causes.
