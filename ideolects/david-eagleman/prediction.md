Prediction, in this framework, is the brain's use of anticipated future sensory or motor consequences to offset the tens-to-hundreds of milliseconds of neural transmission delay between events in the world and their cortical representation. When the environment is sufficiently regular, the system can live slightly “ahead” of raw sensory arrival by expecting what is about to happen rather than waiting for delayed confirmation.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman positions prediction not as the default story of all perception but as one of two complementary delay-compensation strategies—the other being postdiction. Intelligent brains predict when they can; when the world changes unpredictably, prediction fails and the system instead decides what happened retrospectively. That pairing rules out pure predictive accounts of phenomena such as flash-lag, while still treating prediction as a core job of nervous systems. Motor-sensory recalibration sits alongside both strategies as evidence that timing itself is actively rewritten at the perceptual level, not merely forecasted.
