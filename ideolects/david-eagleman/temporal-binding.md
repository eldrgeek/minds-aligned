Temporal binding is the problem—and the solution—of how features and events that arrive in the nervous system at different latencies are nonetheless experienced as ordered, simultaneous, or causally related in a single coherent timeline. Different stimulus attributes and modalities are processed at different speeds, yet psychophysical judgments of temporal order can be far more precise than the raw spread of those neural arrival times would seem to allow.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats temporal binding as a mapping problem between neural time and perceived time, not as mere feature fusion. He highlights an ~80 ms timescale that appears both psychophysically and physiologically: in primate visual pathways, the slowest-to-fastest latency spread driven by luminance contrast is on that order, and he proposes that the brain effectively waits for the slowest relevant signals before committing to a percept. That “wait for the latecomers” strategy rules out any simple isomorphism in which spike-arrival order is perception’s order, and it sits with postdiction and delay compensation as constructive machinery that builds a usable timeline after asynchronous evidence has landed.
