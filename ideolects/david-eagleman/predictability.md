Predictability, in Eagleman’s duration work, is the degree to which a stimulus is expected from recent context—most often whether it is a repeated item in a train versus an unexpected “oddball.” Highly predictable stimuli are judged shorter in duration than physically matched novel ones; the same physical millisecond stretch can therefore feel longer or shorter solely as a function of how surprising the event is.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman uses predictability to pry duration illusions apart from a global “time slowing” story. When an unexpected visual event expands reported duration, concurrent measures of pitch and flicker rate do not co-dilate, so subjective time is not a single entity that speeds or slows; only the duration channel moves. That finding rules out pure attentional “slow motion” accounts as the whole explanation and sets up later claims that felt duration tracks neural response magnitude (and thus coding efficiency) rather than a unitary internal clock.
