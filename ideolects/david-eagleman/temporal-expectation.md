Temporal expectation is the brain’s learned model of when an uncertain event is likely to occur—as when a sprinter waits for a starting cue whose arrival time is drawn from a distribution. In Cui, Stetson, Montague, and Eagleman’s fMRI work, little signal builds during the wait itself; instead, a large post-cue response, especially in supplementary motor area, scales with the cumulative hazard (conditional probability that the cue would have arrived by that wait duration), disappearing when a countdown makes arrival time known and persisting even when no motor response is required.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats temporal expectation as something measurable in the amplitude of a late, post-event signal rather than as sustained anticipatory ramping during the wait. That rules out equating “expecting in time” with a simple clock that accumulates until the go cue, and it separates probability-of-arrival coding from elapsed-time coding and from motor output. Expectation here is a distributional, hazard-like computation that rewrites how strongly the brain tags a cue once it arrives—linking timing, surprise, and readiness without requiring a continuous online countdown in BOLD.
