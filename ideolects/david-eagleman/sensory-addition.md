Sensory addition is the use of a non-invasive channel—typically patterned touch from a haptic device—to give the nervous system a genuinely new kind of information it did not previously receive as a dedicated sense, such as magnetoreception or other engineered “extra” streams. It sits alongside sensory substitution (routing a lost sense, e.g. sound-to-touch for hearing loss) and sensory expansion (widening an existing modality, e.g. detecting electromagnetic bands outside natural vision) as one of three distinct goals for haptic sensory engineering.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman does not collapse every wearable “new sense” story into substitution. He treats addition as a separate design target: the brain’s plasticity is assumed flexible enough to incorporate novel, arbitrarily mapped streams, not only to recover lost channels. That framing rules out the idea that haptic interfaces are useful only as medical prostheses for deficit; it casts them as tools for expanding the human sensorium, and it sets evaluation criteria around learnability and utility of information the species never evolved to sense rather than around restoration of pre-injury performance alone.
