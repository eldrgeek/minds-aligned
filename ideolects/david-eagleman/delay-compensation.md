Delay compensation is the set of strategies by which the nervous system offsets the tens-to-hundreds of milliseconds of lag inherent in sensory transduction, axonal conduction, and multi-stage cortical processing so that perception and action can still align with events in the world. Under this heading fall both feed-forward estimates of what is about to happen and retrospective assignments of what must have happened, including recalibrations that rewrite the felt timing of action and sensation when feedback is consistently delayed.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman frames prediction and postdiction as complementary solutions to the same engineering problem—delay compensation—rather than as rival grand theories of consciousness. Prediction fails in an unpredictably changing world; postdiction then decides what happened after evidence has accumulated. Motor-sensory recalibration is offered as direct perceptual evidence that timing is rewritten at the level of awareness itself, not merely that motor systems plan earlier. The phrase therefore does work that pure “latency” talk does not: it makes neural lag a design constraint the brain actively solves, and it rules out treating every temporal illusion as a mere pipeline artifact with no constructive machinery.
