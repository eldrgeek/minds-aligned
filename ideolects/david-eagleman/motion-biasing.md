Motion biasing is the claim that perceived position of a queried object or flash is systematically shifted by motion signals integrated over a short interval (about 80 ms) after the query is triggered, so localization reports are “pushed” in the direction of subsequent motion. Under this account, flash-lag, flash-drag, flash-jump, and the Fröhlich illusion are different surface forms of one mechanism: post-query motion influences where things are judged to have been, rather than separate timing mismatches among features.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Sejnowski use motion biasing to replace latency-difference and asynchronous feature-binding explanations, in which objects occupy true positions but are misaligned in time. The work they want the term to do is unification plus function: the visual system retrospectively updates location so that, after neural delay, the report is closer to the object’s true physical place. Motion biasing is therefore not a catalog of curiosities but a design principle of dynamic localization—and a spatial cousin of postdiction that rules out pure “different features, different clocks” stories of these illusions.
