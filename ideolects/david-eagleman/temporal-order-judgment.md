A temporal order judgment (TOJ) is a decision about which of two events—often a motor act and a sensory consequence, or two sensory events—occurred first. Repeated exposure to a fixed delay between action and feedback shifts the point of subjective simultaneity, so that observers recalibrate what counts as “before” versus “after” rather than holding a fixed mapping from neural latencies to perceived order.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and colleagues treat TOJs not as passive readouts of arrival times but as the behavioral surface of an actively recalibrating network. Their model pools delay-tuned populations into opponent “before”/“after” units with synaptic scaling, so consistent action–sensation lag automatically rewrites perceived simultaneity—making temporal recalibration a temporal analogue of the motion aftereffect. That frames motor-sensory recalibration and related illusions of reversed causality as normal adaptive dynamics of the same machinery that adjudicates space and time, rather than as failures of a rigid internal clock.
