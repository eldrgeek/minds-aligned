Overlearned sequences are ordinal categories practiced to automaticity early in life—letters, numbers, weekdays, months, and similar ordered lists. In Eagleman’s usage they are not merely semantic sets: words from these categories engage a fronto-temporo-parietal network biased toward the right hemisphere that differs from the networks supporting non-ordinal categories, and they are the inducers most often bound to anomalous secondary percepts in synesthesia.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats overlearned sequences as a privileged computational class, not as arbitrary cultural labels. That framing explains why synesthetic experiences cluster on letters and numbers rather than on random nouns, why ordinal material can map onto unconscious spatial layouts (as in SNARC-like effects), and why some clinical dissociations spare ordinal processing. The term rules out a one-size-fits-all “category” story: the brain’s special handling of drilled ordinal lists is itself a structural clue to how colored sequence synesthesia arises.
