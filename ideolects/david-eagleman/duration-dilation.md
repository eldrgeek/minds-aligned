Duration dilation is the subjective lengthening of an interval—classically the report that time “slowed down” during a life-threatening or highly emotional event—relative to a matched neutral interval of the same physical length. In Eagleman’s freefall experiments, participants retrospectively estimated their own falls as about 36% longer than others’ falls, even though a high-speed visual chronometer worn during the fall showed no improvement in the finest temporal grain they could resolve while falling.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman uses duration dilation to split “subjective time” into separable subcomponents rather than one clock that speeds or slows. The freefall result rules out the folk claim that fright literally increases perceptual sampling rate: there is no online gain in temporal resolution, only a retrospective inflation tied to denser, more salient encoding. Time-slowing is therefore treated as a memory phenomenon—how much of the event can later be reconstructed—not as a real-time stretch of the stream of consciousness, which situates duration dilation among coding-efficiency and recollection accounts of duration rather than among pure clock-rate models.
