Motor-sensory recalibration is the adaptive shift in perceived timing between a voluntary action and its sensory consequence when those events are repeatedly separated by a fixed delay. After exposure to delayed feedback, the nervous system realigns the expected causal mapping so that action and sensation again feel simultaneous; if the artificial delay is then removed, the sensation can subjectively precede the action—an illusory reversal of order.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> For Eagleman this is not a mere curiosity of reaction time but evidence that perceived temporal order is actively edited to preserve a coherent agent-world link. Recalibration demonstrates delay compensation at the perceptual level: the system does not passively accept sensorimotor lag; it rewrites “before” and “after” so causality remains usable. That claim underwrites his broader postdictive stance—timing judgments are plastic products of experience, not fixed readings from a rigid clock—and later neural models treat the same machinery as shared with sensory–sensory temporal order judgments.
