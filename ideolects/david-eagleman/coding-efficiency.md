Coding efficiency, in this usage, is the idea that the felt length of an interval is not a direct readout of clock time but a signature of how much neural energy was spent encoding the stimulus. Repeated or predictable stimuli, which the brain can represent more cheaply (as in repetition suppression), appear subjectively briefer; novel, larger, brighter, or more dynamic stimuli that demand more representational work appear longer even when physical duration is matched.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Pariyadath invert the usual story of duration illusions: many effects that look like “time slowing” for oddballs are better read as contractions of the repeated items, because the neural response shrinks with familiarity. This hypothesis unifies roughly a dozen illusions driven by size, brightness, motion, and flicker under one metabolic/informational principle, and it rules out models in which a single central clock simply ticks faster or slower without reference to stimulus coding cost. Subjective time becomes a byproduct of efficient neural representation rather than an independent timing module.
