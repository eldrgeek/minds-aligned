The proliferation effect is a psychophysical phenomenon in which a brief visual stimulus (a letter, word, object, or face) serially flashed in different locations appears as several simultaneous copies because of persistence of vision. Eagleman’s group treats the number of apparently co-present copies as an implicit measure of how long each flash “lives” subjectively at timescales too short for ordinary explicit duration judgments: fewer apparent copies when the same stimulus is repeated, more when it is novel.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Pariyadath do not treat proliferation as a mere curiosity of iconic memory. They leverage it as experimental evidence that the same repetition-driven neural response reductions that shrink explicit subjective duration also shrink the effective lifetime of brief flashes. That move links a low-level persistence phenomenon to coding-efficiency accounts of duration, rules out the need for a separate slow “clock” reading at these timescales, and turns an everyday visual after-effect into a laboratory readout of how novelty versus predictability sculpts felt time.
