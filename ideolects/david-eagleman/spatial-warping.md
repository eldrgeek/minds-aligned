Spatial warping is a distortion of perceived geometry—straight lines appearing bowed against a background of optic flow, as in a Hering-like configuration—putatively produced by orientation-tuned visual networks so that a dynamic scene is seen closer to where it will be after neural transmission lag (on the order of ~100 ms). Vaughn and Eagleman show the bowing can be driven by optic flow, integrates motion over roughly 80 ms, and is equally strong whether flow precedes or follows the flashed bars, making the warp “peri-dictive” rather than purely predictive or purely postdictive.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats spatial warping as a candidate delay-compensation strategy that is not a precise, on-the-fly model of future positions. The warp direction is the same for inward and outward flow, so if it counteracts latencies it does so as a simple heuristic tuned to typical forward ego-motion, not as a direction-sensitive optimal predictor. That constrains Changizi’s “perceiving the present” idea: any latency-offsetting geometry in vision is a coarse, circumstance-dependent mechanism that works under common conditions, not proof of continuous, accurate temporal extrapolation.
