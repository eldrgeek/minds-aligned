Postdiction is the claim that the conscious percept assigned to a moment is not fixed online at that moment, but is a function of sensory events that arrive in a short window afterward—on the order of tens of milliseconds. In the flash-lag illusion, a flash and a moving object that are physically co-located appear spatially offset; under Eagleman’s account, the location attributed to the time of the flash is interpolated from motion information collected in roughly the next 80 ms, so awareness “looks back” rather than reporting a pure snapshot.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats postdiction as a third framework that rules out both motion extrapolation (a purely predictive fix for neural delay) and simple latency-difference accounts of flash-lag. Visual awareness is neither predictive nor strictly online: the brain often decides what happened retrospectively when the world cannot be predicted, and that retrospective construction is a normal, unifying mechanism of perception rather than a rare laboratory curiosity. In later commentary he pairs postdiction with prediction as complementary delay-compensation strategies, with motor-sensory recalibration as perceptual-level evidence that timing is actively rewritten after the fact.
