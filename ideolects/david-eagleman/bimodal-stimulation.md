Bimodal stimulation is the coordinated pairing of acoustic stimuli with concurrent haptic (or other non-auditory) stimulation so that the two channels land together in time and map systematically in feature space. In Eagleman’s tinnitus research, tones centered on a user’s tinnitus frequency are played while a multi-motor wristband vibrates at spatial locations that depend on tone frequency, aiming to reduce ringing by driving cross-modal plasticity rather than by masking sound alone.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman adopts bimodal stimulation as a practical plasticity protocol, not as a generic dual-task. He moves the non-auditory arm from clinic-bound tongue electrodes to everyday wrist vibration, arguing that frequency-to-place haptic maps can achieve comparable symptom reduction without daily in-office sessions. That usage rules out pure auditory therapies as the only path, treats the skin as a bandwidth-rich input into auditory circuits, and ties tinnitus care to the same sensory-substitution and sensory-addition toolkit he uses for hearing support and human augmentation.
