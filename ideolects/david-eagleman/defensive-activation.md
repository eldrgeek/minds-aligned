Defensive activation is the hypothesis that REM sleep periodically drives the visual system so that visual cortex can hold its territory against rapid cross-modal takeover. Cortical maps maintain their boundaries through ongoing activity; when drive slows or stops—as in blindness—neighboring systems invade on timescales as short as an hour. Dream-related circuitry, on this view, supplies endogenous visual activation through the night so plasticity does not reassign the territory while the eyes are closed.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Vaughn relocate the evolutionary “why” of dreaming away from memory consolidation or threat simulation alone and toward competitive map defense under high plasticity. The theory predicts that species and life stages with greater cortical plasticity should show more REM—a comparative pattern they report across primates—and treats sleep imagery as a byproduct of keeping the visual system “on the air.” It rules out accounts that treat REM as functionless or purely mnemonic, and it ties dream physiology to the same plasticity principles that make sensory substitution possible in waking life.
