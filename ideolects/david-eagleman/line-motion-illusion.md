The line-motion illusion is the percept in which a briefly flashed line, presented all at once, appears to propagate outward from a previously attended or cued location rather than appearing simultaneously along its length. In Eagleman and Sejnowski’s experiments, that apparent direction of growth is not fixed at the moment the line is shown: motion signals delivered after the line has already disappeared can reverse the illusory direction, so the conscious interpretation of the flash depends on information collected in a short poststimulus window.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman uses the line-motion illusion as a clean test against “online” accounts that map first-arriving neural signals directly onto first-perceived events (including the idea that attention simply speeds processing at a locus). If awareness were strictly online, events after the line vanished could not rewrite its direction; their reversal result rules that out and places line-motion with flash-lag under postdiction—awareness as an a posteriori reconstruction over tens of milliseconds, not a live readout of arrival order.
