Sensory substitution is the use of one sensory modality to carry information that would normally arrive through another—for example, converting sound into patterns of vibration on the skin so that people with hearing loss can recover usable auditory content. A haptic device samples the lost channel, encodes it as touch patterns, and relies on the nervous system’s capacity to learn the new mapping so that the substituted stream becomes interpretable behaviorally.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman frames substitution not as a prosthetic gimmick but as evidence that the brain is a flexible pattern interpreter: what matters is structured information delivered with enough bandwidth and consistency for plasticity to latch onto, not which receptor organ delivers it. He systematically distinguishes substitution (replacing a lost sense) from sensory expansion (widening an existing sense, e.g., beyond the visible spectrum) and sensory addition (installing a novel sense such as magnetoreception), treating all three as points on a continuum of deliberate sensory manipulation via haptics rather than as mere clinical repair.
