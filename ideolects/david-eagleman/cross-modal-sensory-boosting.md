Cross-modal sensory boosting is the use of one sensory channel—typically spatially patterned haptic vibration on the skin—to reinforce information that another channel only partially delivers. In Eagleman’s hearing work, a multi-motor wristband detects high-frequency phonemes in real time and maps each to a distinct vibration location, so residual audition is augmented rather than replaced for people with high-frequency hearing loss.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman places boosting between full sensory substitution (replacing a lost modality) and pure sensory addition (delivering wholly new data). The point is not to teach the brain a new language of touch alone, but to supply missing high-frequency speech features that mesh with what the ear still provides—leveraging cortical plasticity so conversation becomes easier without surgery or a cochlear implant. That framing rules out treating every wearable haptic as “substitution,” and it ties applied device design to the same plasticity principles that underwrite his broader sensory-expansion program.
