The window of simultaneity is the range of physical asynchronies within which two events—two flashes, a flash and a bang, or a keypress and a flash—are reported as simultaneous rather than ordered. For passive cross-sensory pairs the window is roughly symmetric (on the order of ±100 ms); when a voluntary motor act is one of the events, the window becomes markedly asymmetric: observers remain highly sensitive to stimuli that precede their action but will call stimuli tens to about 120 ms after the action simultaneous.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats the window not as a fixed property of a central clock but as a shaped decision zone optimized for operant causality—learning which sensory consequences follow one’s acts. Asymmetry after keypresses, impaired interval discrimination in the post-action zone, and later motor-sensory recalibration results all argue that simultaneity is constructed and plastic rather than read off absolute neural arrival times. The window concept therefore rules out pure latency-difference accounts of “when things seem simultaneous” and ties perceived timing to action–effect structure, the same machinery that can reverse the felt order of action and delayed sensation after adaptation.
