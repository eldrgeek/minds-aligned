The oddball effect (or temporal oddball effect) is the illusion that a stimulus differing from a preceding stream of identical items appears to last longer than a standard of equal physical duration. Classically it is described as duration expansion of the unexpected item; the magnitude of the effect grows with how many times the standard is repeated and with how far the oddball deviates from that standard.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Pariyadath invert the folk reading: the oddball does not primarily “stretch time”; the repeated standards shrink in subjective duration as they become predictable, via the same neural dynamics indexed by repetition suppression. The oddball effect thus becomes evidence for coding-efficiency accounts of subjective duration rather than for a global internal clock that speeds up for surprising events. Context sensitivity of the effect further shows that duration judgments track low-level expectation and response amplitude, not a single salience-driven slowing of all temporal experience.
