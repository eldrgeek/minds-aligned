Repetition suppression is the progressive reduction in neural response amplitude when the same stimulus is presented repeatedly. In the duration literature Eagleman draws on, that reduced response is not only an imaging or electrophysiological signature of familiarity; it is proposed as the mechanism by which repeated items come to feel subjectively briefer than matched novel ones.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman treats repetition suppression as the neural bridge between efficient coding and time perception: if subjective duration tracks energy spent encoding a stimulus, then suppressed responses should map onto contracted duration. That links psychophysical oddball and repetition effects to a known cortical phenomenon and predicts that duration contractions should scale with repetition count, stimulus similarity, and experimental context in the same way response suppression does. It rules out accounts in which only high-level expectancy or general arousal resets a central timer, and it makes felt time a side effect of adaptive sensory coding rather than an independent clock module.
