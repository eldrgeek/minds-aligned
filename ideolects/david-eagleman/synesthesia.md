Synesthesia is a condition in which stimulation in one sensory or cognitive stream reliably elicits an additional concurrent experience in another—most famously letters or numbers that carry specific colors—without the concurrent being required by the stimulus for ordinary perception. Eagleman’s program treats it as an empirically bounded phenomenon: concurrents are involuntary, idiosyncratic yet stable within an individual, and distinguishable from ordinary association by quantitative consistency over time.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman operationalizes synesthesia as something you can battery-test rather than accept on self-report alone. The standardized Synesthesia Battery turns the trait into a research object with pass/fail consistency metrics, enabling large-scale genetics, twin, and imaging studies while filtering out loose metaphorical uses of the word. In his framework, synesthesia is a natural experiment in atypical cross-wiring and overlearned-sequence objectification—not a romantic synonym for vivid imagery—and it sits beside sensory substitution as another window onto how the brain builds stable mappings across modalities.
