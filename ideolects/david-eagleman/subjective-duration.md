Subjective duration is the experienced length of an interval—how long a stimulus or event seems to last—when that experience diverges from matched physical duration. In Eagleman's usage it is the dependent measure that shrinks for repeated or predictable stimuli and expands for novel, large, bright, or otherwise energetically costly stimuli, even when objective time is held constant.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman rejects the default assumption that subjective duration is a transparent readout of objective time from a single central clock. He treats it as a signature of coding efficiency: the brain reports something closer to how much neural energy was spent representing the stimulus than to how many milliseconds elapsed on a stopwatch. That move unifies many duration illusions under one principle, separates duration judgments from other temporal measures (pitch, flicker rate, temporal resolution), and reframes “time slowing” reports as changes in a separable duration channel rather than as a global acceleration or deceleration of mental time.
