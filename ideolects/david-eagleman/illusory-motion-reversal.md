Illusory motion reversal is the spontaneous, transient appearance that a continuously rotating object is turning the wrong way—an effect that can occur in steady illumination and that superficially resembles the wagon-wheel stroboscopic reverse under intermittent light. Under the snapshot hypothesis, the visual system would sample the whole field in discrete frames; a mismatch between frame rate and rotation period would then flip perceived direction, just as a movie camera does.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and colleagues use the illusion as a decisive test against discrete global sampling. When two rotating drums are viewed together, almost all reversals strike one drum at a time rather than both at once—the opposite of what a field-wide snapshot (or even a hemifield-specific snapshot) predicts. The preferred account is rivalry among direction-selective motion detectors whose activity approximates a gamma-distributed dwell time, not a cinematographic series of stills. The term therefore does double duty in his toolkit: it names a striking everyday-like illusion and rules out a long-popular discrete-frame model of visual awareness.
