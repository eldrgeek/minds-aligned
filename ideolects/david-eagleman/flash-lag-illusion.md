The flash-lag illusion is the misperception that a brief flash spatially aligned with a continuously moving object appears displaced in the direction of the object's subsequent motion. Under Eagleman's account, the visual system does not report the moving object's true position at the instant of the flash; instead it integrates motion signals collected over roughly the next 80 ms and attributes a spatially biased location to the time of the flash.

<strong>David Eagleman's lens:</strong> Eagleman and Sejnowski treat flash-lag as a spatial localization bias driven by post-event motion, not as a pure temporal lag in which a flash simply takes longer to reach awareness than a moving object. That rules out latency-difference and asynchronous feature-binding accounts that would predict mis-ordered temporal experience of flash and motion under the same conditions. Flash-lag becomes the flagship case of a family of motion-biasing illusions (flash-drag, flash-jump, Fröhlich) that show awareness is built by collecting motion after a query is triggered, compensating neural delay by retrospectively “pushing” objects toward their true later positions rather than by online prediction alone.
