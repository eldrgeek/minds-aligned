Hypothesis search is the process of considering, testing, and revising candidate explanations for how a domain works—ranging from local guesses about a single machine or social situation to broader framework theories. Search can be wide (many alternatives, including low-prior or unconventional ones) or narrow (few alternatives, tightly constrained by current goals and established beliefs). In Gopnik's developmental work, the width of that search is itself a developmental and life-history variable, not a fixed property of "good reasoning."

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik treats wider hypothesis search as a cognitive signature of childhood: extended immaturity supports broader exploration of the space of possible theories before adult pressures favor efficient exploitation of what is already known. That framing rules out equating mature intelligence only with faster convergence on the right answer; childhood "distractibility" or openness can be adaptive breadth of search. It also links Bayesian and sampling accounts of learning to evolutionary life history: different ages may implement different search regimes over the same hypothesis spaces, trading flexibility for precision as goals and caregiving environments change.
