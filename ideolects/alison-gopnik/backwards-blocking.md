Backwards blocking is a pattern of causal inference in which a learner first sees two candidate causes together produce an effect, then later sees one of those candidates alone produce the effect, and consequently reduces belief that the other candidate is a cause. In Gopnik-lab blicket-detector studies, preschoolers show this retrospective discounting from indirect evidence rather than only learning from direct co-occurrence.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Backwards blocking is used as a critical test that children's causal learning is genuinely inferential and roughly Bayesian, not reducible to simple associative strengthening. Pure association predicts that co-occurrence with the effect should keep both candidates strong; backwards blocking requires integrating later solo evidence to revise earlier joint evidence. That result underwrites the broader program of causal maps and Bayes nets: even young children use patterns of evidence to restructure causal beliefs, including cases where the decisive information is indirect.
