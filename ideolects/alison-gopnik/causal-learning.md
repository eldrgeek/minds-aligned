Causal learning is the process by which children recover accurate, abstract representations of what causes what from patterns of evidence—observation, covariation, and especially intervention—rather than from innate fixed causal templates or simple stimulus–response associations alone. In Gopnik and colleagues' framework it is the core developmental achievement that yields usable causal knowledge: models that support prediction, effective action, and reasoning about alternatives.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik treats causal learning as the empirical engine of the child-as-scientist picture. Children are not merely tracking correlations; they deploy specialized learning procedures (formalized with causal maps and Bayes nets) that separate genuine causal structure from spurious association. That rules out reducing early causality to temporal contiguity or operant conditioning, and it departs from adult cognitive science's focus on already-competent causal reasoners: the distinctive claim is that very young children already run powerful structure-learning algorithms, and that those algorithms explain how everyday theories get built and revised.
