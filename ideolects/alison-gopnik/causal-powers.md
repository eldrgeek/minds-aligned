Causal powers are the dispositional capacities objects are taken to have—what they can make happen—rather than only the perceptual properties they currently display. In Gopnik's experimental work, children treat a novel power (for example, the ability to activate a blicket detector) as a basis for naming, grouping, and inductive projection: objects that share a causal power belong together even when they look different, and objects that look similar may be split if only some of them have the power.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik uses causal powers to bind categorization to causal learning: kinds are not mere similarity clusters or pure linguistic conventions, but inferences about what things do. That rules out accounts in which early categories are only appearance-based or only word-driven. It also anticipates the later causal-maps program—powers are the informal counterpart of latent causal structure—and it shows why the blicket detector is not a parlor trick: the machine makes an invisible causal power observable so children can sort and induct on theory-like grounds.
