The child-as-scientist idea holds that infants and young children build knowledge in ways that parallel professional science: they form evidence-based, theoretical representations of the world; they revise those representations rationally in light of new data; and they use active exploration, intervention, and sometimes play as ways of gathering informative evidence.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> For Gopnik this is the organizing analogy of her life's work, not a cute slogan. It unifies theory theory, causal learning, and Bayesian developmental models under one claim: the mechanisms that make science possible are continuous with the mechanisms of ordinary childhood learning. It rules out pictures of the young child as passive associator, pure imitator, or merely incomplete adult. It also departs from taking "scientist" only as institutional role: the comparison is to scientific *thinking*—hypothesis, test, revision—present long before schooling, and often more freely exploratory than adult expertise allows.
