Life history, in Gopnik's usage, is the organism's evolved developmental schedule—length of immaturity, caregiving investment, reproductive timing, and longevity—and the cognitive profile that schedule makes possible. Human life history is distinctive for a long, protected childhood (and often an extended adolescence and grandparental old age), which creates a developmental window in which broad learning can take priority over immediate goal pursuit.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik links life history to the explore–exploit tradeoff: childhood is not merely a waiting period before adult competence but a solution that lets young humans run wider hypothesis search, play, and exploration while caregivers handle exploitation. That rules out treating childhood only as incomplete adulthood or as pure vulnerability. It also reframes cultural learning—imitation versus innovation, neophilia, play—as life-history strategies timed across the lifespan, not as static personality traits, and it situates the child-as-scientist claim inside evolutionary developmental biology rather than only inside cognitive science.
