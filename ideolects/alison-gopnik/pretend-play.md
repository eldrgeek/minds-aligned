Pretend play is children's voluntary engagement with make-believe scenarios—acting as if objects, roles, or outcomes differ from current reality—in ways that systematically explore alternative possibilities. In Gopnik-linked accounts it is not idle entertainment but a behavioral arena in which causal models are flexed: children stage interventions and outcomes that did not occur, thereby exercising the same representational machinery used for counterfactual and causal reasoning.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik and collaborators reframe pretence as continuous with causal learning and the extended human childhood of exploration. Against views that treat pretend play as epiphenomenal or only socio-emotional practice, they propose that pretense recruits causal-model and possibility-reasoning capacities and may scaffold them—especially when culture and caregiving support rich scaffolding. That rules out dismissing pretence as mere false belief or confusion about reality, and it departs from standard developmental psychology that severs play from scientific reasoning: for Gopnik, the child at play is rehearsing the power of possibility that causal cognition requires.
