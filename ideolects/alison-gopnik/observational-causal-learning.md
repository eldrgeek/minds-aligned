Observational causal learning is learning the causal structure of the world by watching other agents act—especially their goal-directed interventions—and using the outcomes of those actions to distinguish real causes from mere correlations. The learner need not perform the critical intervention herself; seeing someone else activate one object rather than another, and seeing what follows, can license a causal inference that guides later choice and action.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> For Gopnik (with Meltzoff and Waismeyer), observational causal learning bridges social cognition and causal Bayes-net style reasoning: other people are not only models to imitate but sources of interventional evidence. That rules out views on which infants only learn causes through their own motor exploration, and views on which social learning is mere mimicry of surface behavior. It also shows how cultural transmission can carry causal knowledge: children inherit not just conventions but information about what makes what happen, because they treat others' intentional interventions as privileged windows onto causal structure.
