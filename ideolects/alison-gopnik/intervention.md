Intervention is the act of deliberately bringing about one event in order to generate, test, or exploit a causal relation to another—doing something to a putative cause rather than only watching events unfold. In the causal-learning framework Gopnik shares with interventionist philosophy and Bayes-net formalisms, interventions are epistemically privileged evidence: they help distinguish genuine causes from spurious correlations and they are what causal knowledge is for when the child acts on the world.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik's developmental program makes intervention the hinge between knowing and doing. Causal representations are not only predictive models; they license effective action, and learning from one's own and others' interventions is a primary route to causal maps. Work such as the "Just do it?" studies further shows that integrating prediction with the possibility of intervening is itself a developmental achievement—toddlers may track predictive relations before they readily act on them. That rules out treating causal knowledge as passive association, and it departs from everyday "intervention" as clinical or educational meddling: here intervention is a formal and psychological primitive of causal cognition.
