Causal maps are abstract, coherent representations of the causal relations among events that children recover from patterns of observation, intervention, and covariation. In Gopnik and colleagues' account they are the content of what children learn about how the world works: not a list of associations, but a structured model of what causes what, usable for prediction, intervention, and counterfactual reasoning.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik treats causal maps as the computational bridge between theory theory and formal learning theory. She and collaborators identify them with Bayes nets (directed graphical causal models), so that children's theory-like knowledge can be learned and revised by well-specified algorithms that use conditional independence, interventions, and prior structure. This rules out reducing causal knowledge to mere temporal association or innate modular templates alone, and it departs from everyday use of "map" as spatial layout: a causal map is an abstract graph of dependencies, the kind of object a young scientist would need in order to act effectively and imagine alternatives.
