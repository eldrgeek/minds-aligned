Difference-making is the capacity to identify which feature or variable in a novel causal system is the one worth changing if you want a particular effect—choosing the causally relevant dimension rather than only learning how a preselected set of variables already map onto an outcome. Faced with many candidate attributes of objects or events, a difference-maker asks what would have to be different for the effect to appear or disappear, and uses that contrast to guide intervention.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> In Gopnik's framework (with Goddu), difference-making highlights a neglected problem of variable choice that standard causal-learning paradigms often hide by handing children the relevant factors. Preschoolers do not merely associate given cues with effects; they actively pick which variable to manipulate when the causal system is unfamiliar. That rules out treating early causal competence as limited to tracking contingencies among experimenter-defined causes, and it deepens the child-as-scientist picture: real scientific work begins with deciding what to vary, not only with estimating the strength of already named causes.
