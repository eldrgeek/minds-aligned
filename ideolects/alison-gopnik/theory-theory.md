Theory theory is the claim that young children's everyday understanding of a domain—especially the mind—is structured like a scientific theory: abstract, coherent, causally organized knowledge that goes beyond sensory data, generates predictions and explanations, and can be revised when evidence conflicts with it. On this view, development within a domain is not mere accumulation of facts or maturation of fixed modules, but the formulation of a succession of increasingly adequate naïve theories.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> For Gopnik, theory theory is not a loose metaphor for "kids think a lot." It is a concrete alternative both to strong nativism (innate, unrevisable domain modules) and to blank-slate empiricism. Theories are the representational medium of conceptual change: they specify ontology, causal laws, and inference patterns, and they are updated by evidence in ways later formalized with causal maps and Bayesian models. What it rules out is treating early knowledge as either hardwired competence or mere association; what it licenses is treating the child as engaged in genuine theory formation and revision across domains such as psychology, biology, and physical causality.
