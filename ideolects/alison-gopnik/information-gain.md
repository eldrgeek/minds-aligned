Information gain is the expected reduction in uncertainty about the world that a learner can achieve by taking an action—looking behind another door, trying another object, asking another question. When more is still unknown, further search yields more information; when little uncertainty remains, the same action yields little.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik and collaborators treat information gain as a real internal motive for exploration, not a metaphor. Even when external rewards are absent or the target is never found, preschoolers persist longer in search environments that still afford high information value. What this rules out is explaining children's exploration only as random play, low impulse control, or chase for tangible payoffs. In her framework it links the child-as-scientist picture to the explore–exploit tradeoff: young learners are designed to chase uncertainty reduction itself, which makes broad causal learning possible before efficient exploitation takes over.
