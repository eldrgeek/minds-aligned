The sampling hypothesis is the proposal that much of the trial-to-trial variability in young children's causal judgments reflects random sampling from a probability distribution over hypotheses, with frequencies matching the child's subjective probabilities given the data, rather than random error, incomplete competence, or unstable preferences alone. On this view, a child who answers differently across similar trials may still be a coherent Bayesian learner who produces one sample at a time from a rich posterior.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Within Gopnik's Bayesian developmental program, the sampling hypothesis converts an apparent liability—preschoolers' inconsistency—into evidence for rational probabilistic inference. It explains how children can cope with vast hypothesis spaces without computing full distributions at every step, and it aligns response variability with algorithms familiar from machine learning. What it rules out is reading every flip-flop as mere noise, impulsivity, or failed determinism; what it departs from in standard developmental scoring is the assumption that the "correct" fixed answer is the only signature of knowledge. Variable responding can be the signature of exploring a hypothesis space.
