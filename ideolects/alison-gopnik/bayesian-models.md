Bayesian models formalize learning as the updating of graded beliefs about hypotheses in light of data: priors over possible structures, likelihoods that link structures to observations, and posteriors that combine the two. In developmental cognitive science they make representational assumptions explicit and specify how children could move from limited evidence to structured knowledge.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik uses Bayesian models as the modern computational embodiment of theory theory and causal-map learning, not as a claim that children consciously calculate Bayes' rule. They explain how abstract causal structure can be learned, how prior knowledge and new evidence interact, and how sampling and approximate inference can produce the variable but rational behavior children show. This rules out both pure empiricism (no structured priors) and fixed nativism (no genuine updating). It departs from "Bayesian" as mere buzzword by tying the formalism to concrete developmental phenomena: causal inference, theory change, and the balance of exploration and exploitation across childhood.
