Theory of mind, in Gopnik's usage, is the child's developing body of knowledge about mental states—beliefs, desires, perceptions, and intentions—and about how those states cause action. It is not a single module that switches on at four, but a coherent, abstract, causally structured theory that young children construct and revise: earlier desire- and perception-based psychologies give way to a belief-desire psychology that can represent false belief and the opacity of other minds.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik insists that the child's theory of mind really is a theory—structured like scientific theories rather than like a fixed modular competence or a mere simulation of one's own experience. That framing rules out pure simulationism and strong modular nativism as complete accounts: if early psychology is theory-like, it should show abstract coherence, explanatory reach, and systematic revision under evidence (including training and conceptual change). Theory of mind thus becomes the flagship case of the theory theory and of the child as scientist applied to the social world.
