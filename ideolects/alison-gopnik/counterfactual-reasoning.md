Counterfactual reasoning is the capacity to evaluate how events would have unfolded if some cause had been different—if one had intervened otherwise, or if a background condition had not held—drawing on an internal model of causal structure rather than on remembered sequences alone. In the Gopnik line of work it is tightly linked to causal model use: once a learner has a causal map, the same representation that supports prediction also supports answers to "what if?" questions.

<strong>Alison Gopnik's lens:</strong> Gopnik and collaborators treat counterfactual reasoning not as a late, language-heavy philosophical skill but as a developmental consequence of powerful causal learning, closely allied with exploratory play and pretence. The distinctive move is to bind counterfactuals to causal models and to childhood immaturity: an extended playful period lets children explore alternative possibilities that adult exploiters would skip. This rules out equating counterfactual thought with mere fantasy disconnected from truth, and it departs from standard adult reasoning research by locating the origins of "what would have happened" in early causal learning and pretend play rather than in mature executive control alone.
