Binocular rivalry is the phenomenon in which conflicting images presented to corresponding regions of the two eyes yield alternating conscious percepts rather than a stable fusion: only one image is typically seen at a time, even though both remain present on the retina. In the experimental tradition Koch works in, it is a method for dissociating physical stimulus from phenomenal visibility.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch uses binocular rivalry as a workhorse for isolating the neural correlates of conscious content: retinal drive can be held roughly constant while the reportable percept switches, so neuronal activity that tracks the dominant percept is a candidate NCC and activity that merely tracks the stimulus is not. Rivalry also sits on a continuum with flash suppression and continuous flash suppression; Koch and colleagues treat CFS not as “stronger rivalry” but as a related interocular-suppression regime with quantitatively deeper threshold elevation. That framing rules out equating consciousness with stimulus presence or early sensory drive alone, and keeps rivalry as a tool for studying visibility, not as a theory of what experience is.
