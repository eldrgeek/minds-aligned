A consciousness meter is a practical, theory-guided assay of the presence and amount of subjective experience—especially in patients or systems that cannot reliably report—rather than a mere checklist of behavioral arousability. In Koch’s presentation of integrated information theory, such a meter is the clinical translation of a quantitative account that starts from experience and asks how much irreducible causal structure a brain currently supports.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch needs a consciousness meter precisely where correlates and verbal report fail: islands of remaining cortex, anesthesia, disorders of consciousness, non-mammalian animals, and engineered systems. The point is not a sci-fi mind-reader for private thoughts, but a graded index of whether “the lights are on” as a matter of physical causal architecture. That rules out equating consciousness with stimulus tracking, command-following, or intelligent output alone, and it puts pressure on any theory that cannot say, in measurable terms, when experience is present, reduced, or absent. The meter idea thus binds IIT’s axioms to bedside and laboratory use without collapsing consciousness into behavior.
