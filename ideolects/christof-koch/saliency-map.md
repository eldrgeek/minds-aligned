A saliency map is a single topographic representation in which multiscale image features—color, intensity, orientation, and related early cues—are combined so that locations stand out according to how conspicuous they are relative to their surround. A dynamical selection process then picks the most salient peak for further analysis and suppresses it so attention can move to the next peak, implementing rapid scene sampling without a full parse of every object.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> For Koch the saliency map is the computational embodiment of bottom-up, stimulus-driven attention—inspired by primate early vision, not a theory of consciousness itself. It separates what grabs processing resources because it is locally unique from what the organism is currently aware of or willfully attending to. That split later feeds his dissociation program: saliency can drive selection and gaze without guaranteeing visual awareness, and awareness can occur for items that were not the strongest saliency peak. The map is therefore a tool for modeling selective vision and for keeping attentional mechanisms conceptually distinct from the neural correlates of conscious content.
