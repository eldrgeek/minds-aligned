Continuous flash suppression (CFS) is a dichoptic presentation technique in which a rapidly changing, high-contrast montage shown to one eye can suppress awareness of a static image shown to the other eye for seconds at a time. The suppressed stimulus still produces retinal and early visual drive, yet the observer typically reports no conscious percept of it.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch and Tsuchiya developed and championed CFS as a tool for isolating conscious visibility from bottom-up sensory presence. Unlike brief masking, CFS yields long, stable periods of invisibility, enabling measurement of afterimages, adaptation, and higher-level processing under suppressed awareness. In Koch's framework it does work classic rivalry and flash suppression do less cleanly: it supports claims that substantial visual processing—and sometimes attentional or integrative effects—can occur without awareness, while still providing a controllable contrast condition for hunting the NCC of a specific image content.
