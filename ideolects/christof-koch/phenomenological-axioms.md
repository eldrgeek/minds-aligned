Phenomenological axioms are the five properties of conscious experience that integrated information theory takes as given rather than derived: intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion. From these axioms the theory obtains corresponding postulates about what physical mechanisms must be like if they are to support consciousness, including a calculus for both the quantity and the quality of an individual experience.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch treats the axioms as the non-negotiable starting point of theory-building: one begins from experience itself, not from behavior, computation, or report, and only then asks which physical systems can realize those properties. That order of explanation is meant to do work that correlates alone cannot—for example, why cortex can be conscious while the cerebellum, despite having more neurons, is not. The axiomatic route rules out functionalist shortcuts in which any system that mimics intelligent output is conscious, and it underwrites Koch's claim that consciousness is a fundamental property of systems with the right causal architecture, graded and possibly widespread in biology yet near-absent in purely feedforward or purely simulated machines.
