Level of consciousness names the global state in which experience is present or diminished as a whole—ordinary wakefulness versus deep sleep, general anesthesia, coma, or generalized seizure—rather than which particular object, color, or thought is experienced. It is the dimension of “how conscious” or “whether conscious,” not “conscious of what.”

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> With Tononi, Koch systematically separates global changes in the level of consciousness from the neural correlates of specific conscious percepts. Studying sleep, anesthesia, and seizures shows which brain conditions extinguish or restore the possibility of experience itself; content paradigms (rivalry, masking, suppression) then ask which local mechanisms specify a given percept once a conscious level is in place. That distinction rules out treating every widespread brain change as an NCC for a content, and rules out treating a vivid local representation as sufficient if the global level has collapsed. It also prepares the ground for IIT-style measures of loss and recovery of consciousness as graded changes in integrated information, not merely as behavioral arousability or stimulus tracking.
