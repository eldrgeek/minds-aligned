Subjective vision is the particular visual experience a person has at a given moment—the conscious percept—rather than the raw retinal stimulus or the motor report that may accompany it. Koch's single-neuron work targets cases such as flash suppression, in which the same retinal input can yield two different conscious percepts, so that neuronal firing can be aligned with what is seen rather than with what hits the eye.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Subjective vision is Koch's operational handle on phenomenal content in the human brain: record from individual medial temporal lobe neurons while awareness flips and ask which cells track the seen image. That strategy insists that correlates of consciousness must follow the report of seeing, not merely stimulus energy or task engagement. It rules out treating early sensory drive as sufficient for experience when the subject denies seeing the stimulus, and it elevates high-level, invariant single-unit selectivity as a candidate stage at which perceptual sensations become explicitly encoded—without yet claiming that medial temporal lobe activity is the full physical substrate of visual awareness.
