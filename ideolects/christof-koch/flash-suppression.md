Flash suppression is a visual paradigm in which one monocular image is rendered invisible by the sudden presentation of a different image to the other eye, even though the original image remains on the retina. In Koch's experimental program it is used as a striking dissociation: the same retinal input can give rise to two different conscious visual percepts, so that neuronal activity can be compared when a stimulus is seen versus when it is suppressed without changing the physical input in the suppressed eye.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch deploys flash suppression as an NCC isolation tool, not as a curiosity about rivalry dynamics alone. Direct recordings in the human medial temporal lobe during flash suppression showed that most visually selective neurons followed the subject's phenomenal percept rather than the retinal stimulus, and that none of the selective neurons responded to a perceptually suppressed image. That result underwrites his broader claim that subjective vision has a localizable neuronal signature separable from mere sensory drive—and it motivates the later continuous-flash-suppression extension as a way to hold that dissociation for much longer durations.
