Primary visual cortex (V1) is the first cortical stage of visual processing, receiving the bulk of retinal input via the lateral geniculate nucleus and extracting local features such as orientation and contrast. In the Crick–Koch program it is the canonical “early” area whose necessity for vision is not in doubt, but whose status as a direct contributor to visual awareness is.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> With Crick, Koch famously argued that we are not directly aware of the neural activity in primary visual cortex: V1 activity is often a prerequisite for later conscious vision, yet the NCC for a specific visual percept should be sought in higher cortical areas whose firing better matches what is seen. That claim rules out the simple identification of consciousness with the first place sensory information becomes cortical, and separates enabling or preprocessing stages from the minimal sufficient correlates of a percept. It also sets a lasting experimental standard for Koch’s NCC work—trace where activity follows the conscious content, not merely the retinal image—and foreshadows later “front versus back” debates about which cortical territories actually specify experience.
