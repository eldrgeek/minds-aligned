An afterimage is a continuing visual sensation that persists after the inducing stimulus has been removed, often opposite in contrast or color to the original. In Koch’s psychophysics it is treated as a measurable readout of how prior stimulation, attention, and conscious visibility shape subsequent perception.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch and colleagues weaponize afterimage duration as a dissociation tool: in a full factorial design, paying attention to a grating shortens its afterimage, whereas consciously seeing the grating lengthens it. That opposing pattern is evidence that selective attention and visual consciousness are not two names for one process. Afterimages also link to continuous flash suppression work, where rendering a stimulus invisible can reduce negative afterimages, showing that visibility—not merely retinal exposure—matters for this aftereffect. The term thus works against the default equation of “attended” with “conscious,” and supports Koch’s broader claim that attention can analyze while consciousness synthesizes, with separable signatures in perception.
