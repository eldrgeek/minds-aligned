Machine consciousness is the question whether artificial systems—robots, uploads, or software running on digital computers—can have genuine subjective experience rather than only intelligent-looking behavior. In Koch's later writing the issue is framed against a simple definition of consciousness as any feeling of being alive, and against a quantitative theory (integrated information) that says which physical architectures can support such feeling.

<strong>Christof Koch's lens:</strong> Koch rejects the common functionalist answer that consciousness is a clever computational hack transferable to any substrate that implements the right program. On his IIT-informed view, even a perfect software simulation of a brain would be fake consciousness: programmable digital computers, however behaviorally impressive, are predicted to experience next to nothing because their causal structure does not realize the requisite integrated cause–effect power. That stance rules out equating consciousness with intelligence, Turing-test performance, or mind uploading as mere pattern copying, while still allowing that some non-biological physical systems could in principle be conscious if built with the right intrinsic causal architecture rather than only emulated in software.
