Behavioral mereology is a theory of parts and wholes in which systems and their constituents are identified not by internal material constitution but by the types of behavior they can exhibit, so that part–whole relations become relations among behavior types and constraints that pass between a system and its subsystems.

<strong>David Spivak's lens:</strong> Spivak (with Fong and Myers) recasts classical mereology in topos-internal, behavioral terms: “part of” is mediated by how constraints and admissible trajectories restrict along interfaces, yielding a modal logic for passing constraints rather than a metaphysics of stuff. This rules out treating composition of open systems as mere spatial containment or set-theoretic membership, and departs from philosophical mereology by making behavior type—not substance—the carrier of the part–whole relation, continuous with Spivak’s sheaf and dynamical-systems program.
