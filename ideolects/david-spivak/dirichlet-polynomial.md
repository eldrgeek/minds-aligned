A Dirichlet polynomial is a contravariant analogue of a polynomial functor on sets: where a polynomial is a sum of covariant representables, a Dirichlet polynomial is built so that each position carries a weight that transforms contravariantly, yielding functors whose algebra is still written in elementary \(+\), \(\times\), and exponential-style notation but dual in variance to ordinary polynomials.

<strong>David Spivak's lens:</strong> Spivak (with Myers) elevates Dirichlet polynomials from a formal dual of \(\mathbf{Poly}\) to an object of study in their own right: the category of Dirichlet polynomials forms a topos, so logic, subobject classifiers, and geometric morphisms become available for the same interface-and-interaction style of thinking that ordinary polynomials support covariantly. This rules out treating “weighted polynomials” as mere generating-function duals without categorical structure, and departs from the standard reading of Dirichlet series in analysis by insisting on a Set-based functorial semantics continuous with Spivak’s broader polynomial program.
